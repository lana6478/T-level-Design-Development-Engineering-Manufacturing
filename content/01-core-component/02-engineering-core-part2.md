# Engineering Core, Part 2

*Design and Development for Engineering and Manufacturing T Level → Core Component → Engineering Core, Part 2*

This page covers the remaining nine of the common core's 17 underpinning knowledge outcomes: mechatronics, control systems, quality management, health and safety, business/financial awareness, professional behaviours, stock and asset management, continuous improvement, and project management. Topic 9 (Mechatronics) maps to [Core Exam Paper 1](04-core-scheme-of-assessment.md); topics 10-17 map to [Core Exam Paper 2](04-core-scheme-of-assessment.md). Subsection numbers mirror the specification.

## 9. Mechatronics

### 9.1 The key components of a mechatronics system

Mechatronics is the integration of mechanical and electronic systems into one functioning system. Mechanical components include gears, cams, linkages, levers and pulleys; electrical/electronic components include sensors, transducers, microprocessors, microcontrollers and actuators; common drive devices include standard electrical motors, servo motors and stepper motors. Students need to know the purpose and function of each component type.

### 9.2 The operation, function and applications of programmable logic controllers (PLC) in mechatronic systems

Covers PLC types (unitary, unitary with modular features, modular) and architecture; operation (sensor signal conditioning, programming); function (process blocks, motor driver integrated circuits, interface devices); and applications (robotic arms, conveyor belts, packaging, SCADA, remote technical units, animatronics). Students need to know the differences between PLC types, how a PLC operates, and the advantages/limitations of PLCs versus alternatives like dedicated integrated circuits or computer-based systems.

### 9.3 The basic principles of hydraulics and pneumatics

Covers the transmission of power, fluid compressibility, and components (valves, pumps, actuators, cylinders, compressors). Students need to know the differences between hydraulic and pneumatic systems and their relative advantages/limitations, and how the components function and appear on schematic diagrams.

## 10. Engineering and manufacturing control systems

### 10.1 Principles and applications of control system theory

Covers inputs, processes (logic gates - AND/OR/NOT, timers, comparators, pulse units, counters, latches), outputs, signals, feedback, open/closed loop systems, transfer functions, summing points, analogue/digital signals, pulse width/amplitude modulation, and how control systems are represented in diagrams - applied to electrical, pneumatic and hydraulic systems measuring pressure, flow, temperature, speed or position.

Students need to be able to produce and explain a system diagram with multiple inputs/outputs and feedback, know the applications and trade-offs of open versus closed loop control (including under/over-damping and time dependency), understand steady-state error, compare analogue and digital signals, and know industry applications including networked communication and data transmission.

### 10.2 How sensors and actuators are used in automation control systems

Covers sensor/actuator types (analogue, digital, active, passive), applications (switches, proximity sensors, laser, vision systems), power sources, and hard-wired versus wireless connections; and their uses in automation (positioning/measuring objects, mechanised lifting and moving, and measurement across electrical, mechanical, thermal, chemical, biological, optical and acoustic/radiation domains).

## 11. Quality management

### 11.1 Quality standards, assurance, control and improvement

Standards: British standards, ISO standards, CE marking, and professional engineering bodies (Engineering Council, IET, IMechE, SOE, CIBSE, IAgrE, IMI, TWI). Assurance and control: a culture of quality, "right first time," ISO 9001, inspection/testing, traceability, document/version control, process capability, statistical process control (SPC), six sigma, and total quality management (TQM). Improvement: failure mode effect analysis (FMEA), Pareto analysis, cause-and-effect diagrams, and quality circles.

Students need to know the function/purpose of standards and how to access them, the roles of the engineering bodies, the difference between quality control and quality assurance, the main requirements of quality standards, why document/version control matters, when SPC beats 100% sampling, six sigma for high-volume manufacture, and the trade-offs of different quality-improvement approaches.

### 11.2 Types and applications of Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) and their purposes

Covers SOP types and applications (manufacturing, quality, maintenance) and purposes (standardising activity, customer satisfaction, safety, training). Students need to know the typical format/content of an SOP, how they're used in different applications, why they're used (consistency, conformance to standards), and how they're produced, implemented and evaluated.

## 12. Health and safety principles and coverage

### 12.1 The main requirements of key health and safety legislation applicable to engineering activities

Key UK legislation students must know the main requirements of, and how it affects their own workplace activities: the Health and Safety at Work Act (HASAWA), Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations, PUWER, PPE Regulations, the Control of Noise at Work Regulations, Manual Handling Operations Regulations, LOLER, Work at Height Regulations, Electricity at Work Regulations, CEMFAW, RIDDOR, and COSHH. Learners don't need to know every legal detail, since company safe systems of work and procedures should already satisfy the legislation - but they should understand why the legislation exists, that it covers every aspect of the workplace, how it keeps them safe, who's responsible for compliance, and the role of safety culture, training and information.

### 12.2 The importance of health and safety practices within the workplace

Covers how legislation affects accident/incident frequency, the importance of mental health and wellbeing at work, who's responsible for compliance (employer, employee, the Health and Safety Executive/HSE), and the implications of non-compliance.

### 12.3 Responsibilities for health and safety

Employee responsibilities include working safely, not attempting untrained/unauthorised tasks, co-operating with the employer, respecting duties placed on the employer or others, and not interfering with or misusing safety provisions. Employer responsibilities include minimising handling/storage/transport risks, providing instruction/training/supervision, maintaining a safe workplace, providing a health and safety policy statement, arranging safety representatives/committees, and protecting visitors, contractors and the public. Students should also know the differences between local, national and global requirements.

### 12.4 Risk assessment

Covers the stages of risk assessment: identifying hazards (HAZOP, HAZID), evaluating risk (likelihood, severity, number of people affected), and implementing control measures using the hierarchy of control (elimination, reduction/substitution, isolation, engineering controls, administration/training/safe systems of work, PPE).

Students need to know engineering hazards (equipment, stored energy, tools, electricity, harmful substances/gases, environments), common industrial injuries, hazard-identification methods, how to evaluate risk, the hierarchy of control, and typical control measures (guarding, machine isolation, PPE such as eye/ear protection, safety shoes, gauntlets, helmets).

### 12.5 Health and safety considerations in specific engineering contexts

Covers considerations (safe systems of work, oxygen use, asphyxiation hazards, heat, moving parts, fire/explosion hazards, fire safety, guarding, manual handling, permit to work, lock out tag out/LOTO, maintenance) across contexts including chemicals, equipment with moving parts, confined spaces, electrical testing, and high-voltage electrical work (generation, distribution, isolation, storage).

### 12.6 Principles and practices relating to environmental legislation and considerations

Key legislation: the Environmental Protection Act, Pollution Prevention and Control Act, Clean Air Act, Radioactive Substances Act, Controlled Waste Regulations, Dangerous Substances legislation, and Hazardous Waste Regulations. Considerations: ISO 14001 and waste disposal. Students need to know the main requirements and purpose of this legislation, who's responsible for compliance, the aims/benefits/consequences of ISO 14001, and waste disposal methods (landfill, reuse, recycling, controlled waste) and their implications.

## 13. Business, commercial and financial awareness

### 13.1 Principles of commercial operations and markets

Covers commercial priorities (profit, stakeholder needs), efficiency, value-added versus non-value-added activity, competition, supply and demand, and markets (local, national, international customers). Students need to know the goals of commercial operations, how organisations address different customer/market needs, how activities are evaluated on quality/cost/time, and the role of R&D and innovation.

### 13.2 Business and commercial practices

Covers legal practices (tendering, contracts, warranties, force majeure, indemnity clauses, liabilities), management practices (resource allocation and planning, staffing, training and development), business models (traditional, agile), and company management systems/policies/procedures. Students need to know how these practices influence engineering organisations and the legislation affecting tendering and contracts.

### 13.3 Financial and economic concepts

Covers financial responsibility, recording transactions, sources of finance (loans, shares, capital), budgets, costs (direct, indirect, overheads), payment terms, revenue, creditors/debtors, cash flow, profit and loss, break-even, assets (depreciation), liabilities, solvency, taxes and rates. Students need to know what each concept means and its implications for running a business.

## 14. Professional responsibilities, attitudes, and behaviours

### 14.1 Professional conduct and responsibilities in the workplace

Covers job descriptions, expected workplace behaviours, personal conduct (reputation, ethical responsibility), levels of accountability in organisational structures (apprentice, operator, management, director), and equality/diversity/accessibility/inclusion. Students need to know the purpose of job descriptions, how conduct affects workplace interactions, how to seek advice, expectations around reputation and ethics, the responsibilities of different organisational roles, and an organisation's duties around equality/diversity/accessibility/inclusion.

### 14.2 Continuous professional development (CPD) and professional recognition

Covers CPD routes (training courses, industry placement, academic study, events/seminars). Students need to know what CPD is and how it motivates staff and improves performance, and the professional standards for engineering set by the Engineering Council.

### 14.3 Human factors within engineering and manufacturing contexts

Covers human factors: human characteristics (physical, mental), workplace design considerations, and human error. Students need to know how human characteristics/capabilities/limitations affect a company and its production, how workplace design affects safety/comfort/productivity, and the causes of human error (insufficient training, fatigue, workload, stress) and how to reduce them.

## 15. Stock and asset management

### 15.1 Stock and inventory management principles and practices

Principles: demand, stock turnover, cost of inventory, redundant stock/write-down, obsolescence, minimum stock levels, supply chain, packaging/storage. Practices: just-in-time, made-to-stock, made-to-order, material requirements planning. Students need to know the purpose of effective stock/inventory management, and the risks/advantages/disadvantages of each practice.

### 15.2 Asset management and control principles

Covers capacity management (manufacturing resource planning, bottlenecks), the stages of asset life cycle management (planning, acquisition, operation and maintenance, disposal), and budgetary control practices (life cycle/whole-life approach, depreciation). Students need to know the purpose and methodology of effective asset management and the trade-offs between capacity-management methods.

## 16. Continuous improvement

### 16.1 Continuous improvement principles and practices

Principles: reflection and evaluation of processes, incremental change, key performance indicators (KPIs), the PDCA cycle (plan, do, check, act), the 8 wastes (transportation, inventory, motion, waiting, excess production, overprocessing, defects, unused talent), lean, and Kaizen. Practices: value stream mapping, visual management, 6S (sort, set in order, shine, standardise, sustain, safety), SMED (single minute exchange of dies), OEE (overall equipment effectiveness), TPM (total productive maintenance), and kanban.

Students need to know how to gather feedback/evidence about performance (including KPI types) to evaluate improvement activity, how the 8 wastes affect engineering performance, and the purpose/methodology/benefits/limitations of each practice.

## 17. Project and programme management

### 17.1 Principles of project management

Covers the project brief, goals and success criteria, the project life cycle (initiation, planning, implementation, monitoring, reporting, evaluation), constraints and risk management (budget, cost, quality, time, safety, resource availability, communication, reputation, changing requirements), and collaborative working (matrix working, collaborative technologies). Students need to know how projects are structured, the documentation/practices needed at each stage, how risk is managed through a project's life (including the role of R&D), and the benefits/limitations of collaborative working.

### 17.2 Roles and responsibilities in projects

Covers roles (stakeholders including clients and regulators, project manager, team members) and responsibilities (communication, monitoring, planning, finance, reporting). Students need to know how each role contributes to a project.

### 17.3 Project planning and control

Planning: resource requirements (time, budget, people, training, communication, production facilities), Gantt charts, critical path analysis (CPA), PERT, managing interdependencies, contingency planning. Control: monitoring reports (budget, quality, cost, time), managing by stages, managing by exception. Students need to know how to identify resource requirements, the benefits/limitations of different planning methods, how to plan using them, and how to monitor, evaluate and review projects to improve future ones.

## Key terms

- **Mechatronics** - the integration of mechanical and electronic systems into one functioning system.
- **PLC** - programmable logic controller, used to automate mechatronic and industrial processes.
- **HAZOP / HAZID** - structured methods for identifying hazards (hazard and operability study / hazard identification).
- **Hierarchy of control** - the preferred order for managing risk: eliminate, reduce/substitute, isolate, apply engineering controls, use administrative controls/training, then PPE as a last resort.
- **KPI** - key performance indicator, a measure used to track and evaluate performance or improvement.
- **PDCA** - Plan, Do, Check, Act - a continuous-improvement cycle.
